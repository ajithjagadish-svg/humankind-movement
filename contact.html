Frequently asked questions

What kind of coaching does Humankind Movement offer?

1:1 and group coaching for general wellbeing, mobility, and injury recovery; prepartum and postpartum recovery coaching; corporate and community wellness programs; movement coaching for neurotypical and neurodivergent individuals of all ages; and speaking or workshops.

What's the difference between a personal trainer and a movement coach?

A personal trainer typically designs and leads workout programs focused on strength, form, and intensity. Movement coaching starts a level deeper: assessing breath, coordination, and how tissue and skeletal structure are actually working together, since that's usually the real cause behind pain, injury, or feeling unstable, not a lack of strength. Ajith Jagadish is a certified Human Biomechanics Specialist, and the approach is built around correcting coordination first, then building strength and capacity on top of that foundation.

Most coaching happens online for individuals across the globe, alongside dedicated programs for corporates and communities. In-person coaching is also available on a limited basis for clients based in Bangalore, subject to availability — get in touch and mention your location to check what's possible.

What is the free postpartum recovery guide, and is it evidence-based?

It's a free guide on the gap between medical clearance and actually feeling ready to move again, built from published research, with an endorsement from Dr. Teji Dawane, MBBS, MS (OBG), Head of Department & Lead Consultant, Obstetrics & Maternal Medicine at Medicover Women & Children Hospital, Bengaluru. It deliberately avoids bounce-back pressure or generic timelines.

When can I start running again after having a baby?

Most healthcare providers give a general clearance around six weeks, but that's checking for immediate complications, not confirming your body is ready for running specifically. The evidence-based guidance points closer to twelve weeks, and even then, only once you can move through basic strength and control checks without symptoms like leaking, pain, or heaviness. Clearance and readiness are two different things — the free guide covers the full timeline in detail.

Is diastasis recti dangerous, or does it go away on its own?

Diastasis recti isn't dangerous on its own, but it's a sign your core isn't yet coordinating the way it should, and it often persists without focused work. For some people the gap narrows with time and daily movement; for many it needs deliberate breath and coordination work, not just time. The width of the gap alone doesn't predict how much it affects you — what matters more is whether your breath, deep core, and pelvic floor are working together under everyday load.
