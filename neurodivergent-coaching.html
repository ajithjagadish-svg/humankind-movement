Neurodivergent population

I adapt the system to the child

Most movement programs ask children to adapt to the system. This one works the other way around. Ajith designs movement coaching for neurodivergent children — in 1:1 sessions and group settings — built from biomechanics, nervous system understanding, breathwork, and lifestyle.

A note on language: this page is written differently from the rest of this site on purpose. It stays concrete, because that's what the work requires — not because the ideas are simpler.

What this looks like

A program built from six things, per child

  • Physical capability
  • Nervous system regulation
  • Behavioural cues
  • Attention span
  • Emotional readiness
  • Environmental factors

Even in group sessions, every child receives an individual pathway.

Where this came from

Individualising within complexity

Before working with children, Ajith coached multiple adults online at once — different goals, different injuries, different needs, all on one screen. That experience taught him how to individualise within complexity, and that skill now shapes how he coaches children.

He works two ways: 1:1 sessions for deeper individual support, and group sessions where children still receive individual attention.

A specific example

When instructions and repetition don't work

A 16-year-old needed physical guidance to move from point A to point B. Instructions didn't work. Repetition didn't work. After observing him closely, Ajith tried something simple — he handed him a resistance band. He held it, and walked independently. The task didn't change. The entry point did.
What progress looks like here

Not a smaller version of an adult goal

Progress in this program isn't measured against what progress looks like in general adult coaching, and it isn't softened into generic language about "inclusion" either. It's specific: a child engaging with a movement instead of resisting it is progress. A child waiting for their next session is progress. Parents report their children wait for sessions. Educators notice changes outside training.

What began as intuition has since been taught to another coach, who now independently works with children using the same approach.

Working with caregivers

Alongside families, not around them

Movement is never just physical — it's often the doorway to everything else. Because of that, this work happens in coordination with parents and educators where relevant, not in isolation from them. What's noticed at home and at school shapes what happens in the next session, and what happens in the session gets fed back.
