I get a version of the same question from parents of autistic and ADHD kids more than almost anything else. Will movement actually help with focus, or am I just tiring my kid out for an hour so the evening is easier? It's a fair question, and until recently the honest answer was "probably, but the evidence is thin."

That's shifted. A meta-analysis published this year pooled 17 randomized controlled trials and 626 children with autism spectrum disorder, comparing kids who went through structured exercise programs against those who didn't, specifically measuring executive function, the mental skills involved in planning, switching between tasks, and holding information in mind.1

What moved the needle

The pooled result showed a moderate, statistically significant improvement in executive function for kids who did structured exercise, and the effect wasn't tied to one narrow type of movement. Traditional sports, mind-body practices, exergaming, and sensory integration training all showed up in the included trials. The clearest pattern was duration: programs that ran ten weeks or longer produced stronger results than shorter ones.

That duration finding matters more to me than the headline number. It tells me this isn't about one good session or one clever drill. It's about a body getting repeated, structured practice with movement over real time, the same way any skill gets built.

What This Kind of Study Can and Can't Tell Us

Seventeen trials and 626 children sounds like a lot, but it works out to about 37 children per trial. Trials that small can swing a lot from one to the next. "Moderate" is also an average. It means the group did better, not that every child did. Some children will gain a lot and some a little, and a meta-analysis cannot tell you which yours will be.

The review also grouped very different activities together. For families that is good news, because it means there is no single right activity. For anyone hoping to be told exactly what to do, it is a limit.

Why this matches what I see in sessions

I'm a coach, not a clinician, and I'm not diagnosing or treating anything. What I can say from years of working with neurodivergent kids and adults is that executive function struggles rarely show up as a standalone problem in a session. They show up tangled together with regulation, meaning a kid who can't sequence a three-step drill is often also a kid whose nervous system hasn't settled yet. The movement work isn't separate from the regulation work. It's often the same work.

What This Could Look Like at Home

Choose movement your child can tolerate and even enjoy, because a programme that stops in week three cannot show ten-week benefits. Think in blocks of weeks, not single sessions, and treat ten weeks as a planning horizon, not a promise. Start from the senses: some children need quiet, or dim light, or a clear beginning and end. Give short, concrete instructions one step at a time, and let your child see the whole sequence first. And tell your child's doctor or therapist what you are trying, since they know things about your child that I do not.

This research gives me more confidence recommending consistent, longer-arc movement practice to families, rather than treating exercise as a one-off outlet for extra energy.
