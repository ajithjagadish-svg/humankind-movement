A client once described her knee pain as random. Fine on some mornings, sharp on others, with no pattern she could point to. It wasn't random. It was the difference between a joint that had already been moving that day and one that had been sitting still, and stairs have a way of exposing exactly which one you're standing on.

The advice most people get for knee pain on stairs is about technique. Turn your toes out. Take wider steps. Hold the rail. Lead with the stronger leg. All of that can genuinely help, and I use versions of it with clients regularly. But it treats the stairs as the problem to be solved, when for a lot of people the more useful question is what state the knee was in the moment before they started climbing.

Why Modifying How You Climb Isn't Enough

Cartilage doesn't have its own blood supply. It depends on the joint moving to spread synovial fluid across its surface and keep it lubricated, the same way a hinge needs to move a little before it stops squeaking. A joint that has been static for a while, first thing in the morning, after a long flight, after hours at a desk, hasn't had that fluid redistributed yet. The first few steps of a climb often load the joint before that catches up, which is part of why the first flight can hurt more than the fifth.1

The knee that hurts on the first few stairs isn't necessarily weaker than the one that doesn't. It's just less prepared. Changing how you climb doesn't address that. It just asks a joint that's still cold to do the same job more carefully.

A Simple Way to Prepare the Knee Before You Move

What I have clients do instead, before a climb that they know will bother them, is something much smaller than an exercise. Lying or seated, lift the leg slightly off the ground, no real height needed, and slowly straighten and bend the knee a handful of times, unweighted. Do it again with the toes rotated in, and again rotated out. It takes under a minute. It isn't treatment for arthritis and it won't fix a joint that needs medical attention. It's closer to what stretching your fingers does before you start typing, a small movement that asks the joint to move through its range before it has to carry weight.

Most people notice the difference on the very next flight, not because anything got stronger in thirty seconds, but because the joint arrived at the stairs already moving instead of being asked to start moving and bear weight at the same time.

How to Tell If It Is a Warm-Up Problem

Here is a rough test. Pay attention to what the pain does over the first five to ten minutes of moving around. If the knee is stiff and sore at first and then eases as you warm up, that fits a joint that needed to move. If it gets worse the longer you use it, or it is just as bad on the fifth flight as on the first, that is a different story and worth having looked at. This is a rule of thumb, not a diagnosis.

Long sitting counts as well. After a long meeting or a long drive, the same minute of gentle movement can help before you take the stairs.

When to Get the Knee Checked

See a doctor or physiotherapist if the knee swells, feels hot, locks, gives way, or wakes you at night, or if the pain began after a fall or a twist. Those are not things to prepare your way through.

Two Ways to Deal With a Movement That Hurts

This is the same distinction I come back to across almost everything I coach. There are two ways to deal with a movement that hurts. You can change the movement, or you can prepare the body to do the movement it was already going to do. Both are legitimate. But if the only tool you've ever been handed is the first one, wider steps, a rail, going slower, you will keep managing the pain without ever finding out how much of it wasn't really about the stairs at all.
